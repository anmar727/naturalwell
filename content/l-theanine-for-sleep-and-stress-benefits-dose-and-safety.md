## Quick answer

L-theanine is an amino acid found naturally in green and black tea. It is sold as a supplement, often marketed for relaxation, stress and better sleep.

- **It may modestly improve how people rate their sleep.** A 2025 review of 19 trials found small improvements in self-reported sleep quality, time taken to fall asleep and daytime tiredness.
- **Measured sleep did not change.** The same review found no clear effect on objectively recorded sleep, total sleep time or how efficiently people slept.
- **The research has gaps.** More than half of the trials tested products that mixed L-theanine with other ingredients, and doses varied widely.
- **It appears well tolerated.** Trials have not found serious side effects, and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has raised no objections to its use as a food ingredient. Safety in pregnancy is not established.

L-theanine may be worth trying for mild, short-term sleep or stress problems in healthy adults, but it is not a treatment for insomnia disorder or anxiety disorders.

## What the research says

**A 2025 meta-analysis on sleep.** D'Cunha, Bulman and colleagues at the University of Canberra reviewed L-theanine and sleep in *Sleep Medicine Reviews*. They included 19 randomised controlled trials with 897 participants in total, using doses from 50 mg to 1,000 mg and lasting from a single night to eight weeks. Pooling the results showed small but statistically significant improvements in three self-reported measures: how long it took to fall asleep (10 studies), daytime dysfunction such as sleepiness (9 studies) and overall sleep quality (12 studies). There was no significant improvement in objectively measured time to fall asleep, sleep efficiency, sleep duration or sleep disturbances. The authors noted that 11 trials used multi-ingredient products rather than L-theanine alone, and that the best dose and duration are still unknown. They concluded that further well-designed trials are needed.

**A trial in healthy adults (2019).** Hidese and colleagues, in *Nutrients*, gave 30 healthy adults 200 mg of L-theanine or a placebo each night before sleep for four weeks, then switched them over after a two-week break. Sleep scores improved during the L-theanine period, but the overall difference compared with placebo was not statistically significant. Some parts of the sleep score, such as sleep disturbance and use of sleep medicines, improved more than with placebo, and time to fall asleep was borderline. Stress-related measures such as trait anxiety and low mood also improved during the L-theanine period, but not significantly more than with placebo. No side effects were apparent and nobody dropped out.

**Anxiety and stress.** Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center's herb database describes the results for anxiety as mixed. It notes small studies suggesting better sleep quality, including in boys with ADHD and in people with cancer who had insomnia, and states that other claimed uses have not been studied well enough.

**Where this leaves things.** Taken together, the evidence suggests a small effect on how people feel they slept, which is consistent with a mild calming action, rather than a strong sleep-inducing effect. That is why it is rated as limited here.

## How to use it

There is no official dose for sleep or stress. The figures below come from studies and food safety reviews.

- **Doses studied:** sleep trials used between 50 mg and 1,000 mg a day. The 2019 trial used 200 mg taken before bed, a dose the Sleep Foundation also mentions. Starting at the lower end makes sense.
- **Timing:** in the 2019 trial it was taken shortly before going to sleep.
- **Give it a fair trial:** studies lasted up to eight weeks. If there is no benefit after a few weeks, it is unlikely to help.
- **From tea:** one serving of tea contains roughly 25 to 60 mg of L-theanine, according to the Sleep Foundation. However, most tea also contains caffeine, which can disturb sleep, so tea in the evening is not an ideal way to get it.
- **Choose products carefully:** many "sleep" or "calm" supplements combine L-theanine with melatonin, GABA, magnesium or herbs. Check the full label so you know what you are taking. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) warns that labels such as "standardized" or "certified" are not proof of quality.
- **Build good sleep habits:** NCCIH notes that the most strongly supported treatment for long-term insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), along with a regular sleep schedule, less caffeine and alcohol late in the day, and a cool, dark, quiet bedroom.

> L-theanine supplements are not a substitute for medical care. If poor sleep lasts for weeks, affects your daily life, or comes with loud snoring or gasping at night, talk to a doctor. Do not stop or change any prescribed sleep or mental health medicine without advice.

## Possible side effects and interactions

- **Side effects:** clinical trials have generally found L-theanine safe and well tolerated. An FDA letter from 2026 summarises human studies using up to 900 mg a day for up to 12 weeks with no serious adverse events. The 2019 trial reported no apparent side effects.
- **Caffeine-related effects from tea:** if you get L-theanine from tea, too much tea can cause headaches, irritability, stomach upset and trouble staying asleep.
- **Sedatives and sleep medicines:** Memorial Sloan Kettering warns that L-theanine may add to the drowsiness caused by medicines such as lorazepam, diazepam and zolpidem.
- **Cancer treatment:** laboratory studies suggest L-theanine might change the activity of some chemotherapy drugs, so people having chemotherapy should talk to their cancer team first.
- **Other medicines:** interactions with many medicines have not been well studied. NCCIH advises telling your health care providers about any supplement you use.

## Who should avoid it

- **Pregnant or breastfeeding women:** safety has not been established, so it is best avoided unless a doctor advises otherwise.
- **People taking sedatives or sleeping tablets,** because of the risk of extra drowsiness.
- **People having chemotherapy,** unless their cancer team agrees.
- **Children:** NCCIH advises getting professional advice before giving any sleep supplement to a child.
- **Anyone allergic or sensitive to green tea.**

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or other health professional if:

- you snore loudly, or gasp or snort during sleep, which can be signs of sleep apnoea
- you struggle with sleepiness during the day
- poor sleep continues for several weeks or is affecting your work, mood or relationships
- stress or anxiety feels overwhelming, constant or out of proportion, or comes with low mood that does not lift
- you are thinking of combining L-theanine with prescribed medicines

## Frequently asked questions

### Does L-theanine make you sleepy?

It is not a sedative in the way sleeping tablets are. Studies suggest it may help people feel more relaxed and rate their sleep a little better, but it did not clearly change measured sleep time.

### Can I take L-theanine every night?

Trials have used it nightly for up to eight weeks without serious problems. There is little information on longer use, so it is sensible to review whether it is helping after a few weeks and speak to a pharmacist if you plan to take it long term.

### Is drinking green tea the same as taking L-theanine?

Not quite. A cup of tea has much less L-theanine than most supplements and also contains caffeine, which can keep people awake.

### Can I take L-theanine with melatonin or magnesium?

Many products combine them, but these mixtures have not been well tested for safety together. If you take other sleep aids or medicines, check with a pharmacist first.

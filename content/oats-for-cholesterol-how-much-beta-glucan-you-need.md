## Quick answer

Oats are one of the few foods with solid evidence for lowering cholesterol, but the effect is modest and depends on the amount.

- **The active ingredient is beta-glucan**, a type of soluble fibre found in the cell walls of the oat grain.
- **About 3 g of oat beta-glucan a day** is the amount linked to a cholesterol benefit. European and US food regulators both accept this.
- **The typical drop is small**: around 5 to 7% lower LDL ("bad") cholesterol on average, with no clear change in HDL ("good") cholesterol or triglycerides.
- **Oats work best as part of a wider diet change**, not as a replacement for medicine your doctor has prescribed.

## What the research says

Oat beta-glucan is one of the most studied food ingredients for heart health, and the findings have been fairly consistent for decades.

**Regulators have reviewed the evidence.** In 2010 the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded that oat beta-glucan lowers LDL cholesterol, and that a daily intake of at least 3 g is needed for the effect. EFSA judged that this amount can reasonably be eaten as part of a balanced diet. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has allowed a heart health claim for beta-glucan from whole oats since 1997.

**A 2014 meta-analysis gives the clearest numbers.** Whitehead and colleagues pooled 28 randomised controlled trials, published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, in which people ate at least 3 g of oat beta-glucan a day. Compared with control diets, LDL cholesterol fell by about 0.25 mmol/L and total cholesterol by about 0.30 mmol/L. HDL cholesterol and triglycerides did not change significantly. Eating more than 3 g a day did not add a clear extra benefit.

**An earlier review found similar results.** A 2011 review in *Nutrition Reviews* by Othman and colleagues concluded that 3 g or more a day may lower total and LDL cholesterol by roughly 5 to 10% in people with normal or raised cholesterol. Across studies, eating oats was linked with about 5% lower total cholesterol and 7% lower LDL cholesterol.

**How it seems to work.** Beta-glucan dissolves in the gut and forms a thick, gel-like liquid. Harvard's Nutrition Source explains that it binds cholesterol-rich bile acids in the intestine and carries them out of the body. A 2019 review in *Frontiers in Nutrition* links this viscous gel to the cholesterol effect.

**Not all oat products are equal.** The same review notes that larger, more intact beta-glucan molecules work better than heavily broken-down ones. In one large trial, oat products with high or medium molecular weight beta-glucan lowered LDL cholesterol, while a low molecular weight version did not. Heavy processing, baking and the rest of the food can all affect this, and many trials did not report it, so the effect of a particular snack bar or drink is hard to predict.

**What this means in practice.** The evidence that oat beta-glucan lowers LDL cholesterol is strong, but the effect is small. Harvard's Nutrition Source also points out that studies of whole-grain diets in general have been mixed, so oats should be seen as one helpful piece of a heart-healthy diet rather than a treatment in themselves.

## How to use it

The goal is to get about 3 g of oat beta-glucan across the day, every day. Some practical points:

- **Read the label.** Beta-glucan content varies between products. Where the pack lists beta-glucan per serving, use it to add up your daily total.
- **Choose less processed oats when you can.** Harvard's Nutrition Source lists oat types from least to most processed: oat groats, steel-cut, Scottish (stone-ground), rolled and quick or instant oats. Less processed oats are digested more slowly and have less effect on blood sugar.
- **Watch the extras.** Harvard's Nutrition Source notes that many instant oat brands are sweetened or flavoured, so check the ingredients for added sugar. Plain oats with fruit are a simple alternative.
- **Spread oats across meals.** Porridge, overnight oats made with rolled oats, or oat bran stirred into yoghurt all count towards the daily total.
- **Make it a habit.** The benefit in the trials came from eating beta-glucan every day. An occasional bowl of porridge is unlikely to change a blood test.
- **Combine it with other changes.** A British Dietetic Association fact sheet notes that soluble fibre from oats, barley, linseeds, beans and lentils, together with plant protein and 2 g a day of plant stanols or sterols, can lower LDL cholesterol by up to 25% when combined in one eating pattern. Cutting saturated fat matters more than cutting foods that contain cholesterol.

Mayo Clinic notes that 5 to 10 g or more of soluble fibre a day, from all sources, can lower LDL cholesterol. Oats are an easy way to cover part of that.

> Oats can support a cholesterol-lowering diet, but they do not replace cholesterol medicines such as statins. If you have been prescribed one, keep taking it and talk to your doctor before changing anything.

## Possible side effects and interactions

Oats are an ordinary food and are safe for most people. The main side effects come from adding a lot of fibre quickly.

- **Wind, bloating and cramps.** Mayo Clinic advises increasing fibre gradually over a few weeks so your gut can adjust, because adding too much too fast can cause gas, bloating and cramping.
- **Fluids.** Drink plenty of water as you eat more fibre. Fibre works best when it can absorb water, which also keeps stools soft.
- **Gluten cross-contact.** Oats do not naturally contain gluten, but most are grown or processed alongside wheat, barley and rye, so ordinary oats can be contaminated.

No important medicine interactions are listed in the sources reviewed for oats eaten as food. Concentrated beta-glucan supplements are a different product, so check with a pharmacist or doctor before taking one alongside regular medicines.

## Who should be careful

- **People with coeliac disease.** The NHS advises discussing oats with your healthcare professional first and choosing only oats labelled gluten-free. Coeliac UK says most people with coeliac disease can tolerate gluten-free oats, but a very small number still react to avenin, a protein in oats.
- **People with gluten sensitivity.** Harvard's Nutrition Source advises choosing oats that are specifically labelled gluten-free.
- **People whose high cholesterol runs in the family.** The NHS notes that high cholesterol can be inherited (familial hypercholesterolaemia) and that some people need medicine as well as diet changes, so oats should not delay a medical check.

## When to see a doctor

High cholesterol usually causes no symptoms, and the NHS says a blood test is the only way to know your level. Speak to a doctor or practice nurse:

- If you have never had your cholesterol checked, especially if you are over 50, overweight, smoke, drink a lot of alcohol or eat a diet high in fatty foods, which the NHS lists as factors that make high cholesterol more likely.
- If high cholesterol runs in your family, since the inherited form can start at a young age.
- If you have been eating more oats and making other changes for a few months and want to know whether your levels have improved.
- Before stopping or reducing any cholesterol medicine.

Call emergency services straight away for chest pain or any signs of a heart attack or stroke. These need urgent care, not a home remedy.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long do oats take to lower cholesterol?

The trials gave people oat beta-glucan every day over a set period, so expect gradual change rather than a quick result. Ask your doctor when a repeat blood test makes sense after you have changed your diet.

### Is oat milk as good as oats?

Not necessarily. Processing can break beta-glucan down, and amounts vary between products, so check the label. Plain oats are a simpler way to know what you are getting.

### Are instant oats as good as rolled or steel-cut oats?

Harvard's Nutrition Source says the nutrient content of steel-cut and instant oats is similar, but instant oats raise blood sugar faster. Plain instant oats still contain beta-glucan. Avoid sweetened sachets where possible.

### Can I eat oats if I am taking a statin?

Oats are an ordinary food and fit well with a cholesterol-lowering diet, so most people taking a statin can eat them as usual. They work alongside medicine; they do not replace it. If you have questions about your own diet and medicines, ask your doctor or pharmacist.

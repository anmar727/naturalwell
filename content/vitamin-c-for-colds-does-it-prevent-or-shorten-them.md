## Quick answer

Vitamin C is the best-known cold remedy, but the research gives it a modest role.

- **It does not prevent colds in most people.** In large trials, people taking it daily caught colds about as often as people who did not.
- **Taken regularly, it slightly shortens colds.** On average, colds were about 8% shorter in adults and 14% shorter in children who were already taking vitamin C before they fell ill.
- **Starting it after you feel ill shows no consistent benefit.**
- **People under extreme physical strain**, such as marathon runners, had about half the risk of catching a cold when taking it regularly.

Food and modest supplements are safe for most people; very high doses mainly add stomach upset.

## What the research says

The main source of evidence is a **Cochrane systematic review by Hemilä and Chalker (2013)**, which pooled placebo-controlled trials that used at least 200 mg of vitamin C a day.

**Prevention.** In 29 trial comparisons involving more than 11,000 people from the general population, daily vitamin C did not reduce the number of colds people caught.

**Heavy physical stress.** Five trials included about 600 marathon runners, skiers and soldiers on exercises in subarctic conditions. In these groups, regular vitamin C roughly halved the risk of catching a cold. The US National Institutes of Health (NIH) Office of Dietary Supplements notes that the doses in these trials ranged from 250 mg to 1 g a day.

**Length of colds.** In 31 comparisons covering almost 10,000 cold episodes, people who took vitamin C regularly had shorter colds: about 8% shorter in adults and 14% shorter in children. Children taking 1 to 2 g a day had colds about 18% shorter. Symptoms were also somewhat milder. Germany's independent health information service, InformedHealth.org, puts this in everyday terms: a ten-day cold might last about nine days instead.

**Starting it when a cold begins.** Seven comparisons (about 3,200 episodes, none in children) tested vitamin C started after symptoms began and found no consistent effect on cold length or severity. A few individual trials reported some benefit, so the authors called for more research, but the evidence does not show that taking it at the first sniffle reliably helps.

**The authors' conclusion** was that routine daily vitamin C is not justified for the general population as a way to prevent colds. The NHS takes a similar line, saying there is little evidence that vitamin C prevents or shortens colds.

## How to use it

**Food first.** The NHS sets the daily amount for adults aged 19 to 64 at 40 mg, while the US recommends 75 mg for women and 90 mg for men, plus an extra 35 mg for people who smoke. A varied diet usually provides this easily. According to the NIH, typical amounts include:

- Half a cup of raw red pepper: about 95 mg
- One medium orange: about 70 mg
- One medium kiwifruit: about 64 mg
- Half a cup of cooked broccoli: about 51 mg
- Half a cup of sliced strawberries: about 49 mg

The body cannot store vitamin C, so it needs a regular supply from food.

**If you choose a supplement.** The Cochrane trials used at least 200 mg a day, taken regularly rather than only when ill. If you want to try it:

1. Take it **daily and consistently**, since the small benefit on cold length was seen in people already taking it before they became ill.
2. Keep the total **below 1,000 mg a day** for routine use. The NHS says amounts under this are unlikely to cause harm, and larger doses are poorly absorbed: above 1 g a day, less than half is absorbed and the rest is passed in urine.
3. Count vitamin C from **multivitamins and fortified drinks** as part of your total.

**Being realistic.** Vitamin C does not replace the basics the NHS recommends for colds: rest, plenty of fluids and simple pain relief if needed.

> Very high doses of vitamin C do not give extra protection against colds. For adults, the upper limit from all sources is 2,000 mg a day, and children's limits are much lower, so check the label and never give adult-strength supplements to a child.

## Possible side effects and interactions

In the trials, people taking vitamin C reported no more side effects than those on placebo. Problems mainly appear at high doses:

- **Stomach upset.** More than 1,000 mg a day can cause stomach pain, diarrhoea and wind. These usually stop when the supplement is stopped.
- **Kidney stones.** High intakes can raise levels of oxalate and uric acid in urine. The evidence is mixed, but the concern is greatest for people who already have a tendency to form oxalate stones.
- **Iron overload.** Long-term high doses could worsen iron overload in people with hereditary haemochromatosis. This is not considered a concern for healthy people.
- **Cancer treatment.** Whether high-dose antioxidants interfere with chemotherapy or radiotherapy is debated, so the NIH advises people having cancer treatment to talk to their oncologist before taking vitamin C supplements.
- **Statins and niacin.** Vitamin C combined with other antioxidants may blunt the rise in "good" HDL cholesterol from niacin taken with simvastatin.

**Teeth.** Vitamin C is ascorbic acid, and chewable tablets are acidic. A 2024 laboratory study of children's chewable vitamin supplements found all of them were acidic and most caused enamel erosion, with vitamin C products causing more enamel loss than multivitamins. Laboratory results do not fully reflect what happens in the mouth, but it is one reason to prefer tablets that are swallowed whole over chewable forms taken repeatedly through the day.

## Who should be careful

- **Children:** use only child-specific products at the dose on the label. Upper limits range from 400 mg a day for ages 1 to 3 up to 1,800 mg for teenagers.
- People with a **history of kidney stones**
- People with **haemochromatosis** or other iron overload conditions
- Anyone having **chemotherapy or radiotherapy**
- People taking several supplements, who may be getting more vitamin C than they realise

If you take prescribed medicines, check with a pharmacist before starting a high-dose supplement.

## When to see a doctor

The NHS says most people start to feel better from a cold within about one to two weeks, and that antibiotics do not help. Vitamin C will not change when you need medical advice. See a doctor if:

- A **high temperature lasts more than three days**, or your temperature is very high or you feel hot, cold and shivery
- You feel **short of breath** or have **chest pain**
- Your symptoms **get worse**, or do not improve after **10 days**
- You have a **cough lasting more than three weeks**
- You have a **long-term condition** such as diabetes or a heart, lung or kidney condition, or a **weakened immune system** (for example from chemotherapy), and develop cold symptoms
- You are worried about your **child's** cold symptoms

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I take vitamin C as soon as I feel a cold coming on?

It is unlikely to cause harm at modest doses, but trials of this approach did not show a consistent benefit. The small effect was seen in people already taking it regularly.

### Is vitamin C from food as good as a supplement?

For meeting daily needs, yes. The cold trials used at least 200 mg a day, several times the daily recommendations of 40 to 90 mg, so a typical diet may not reproduce the small effect on cold length.

### Does more vitamin C work better?

Not in a simple way. Absorption drops sharply above 1 g a day, and higher doses mainly add to the risk of stomach upset. Children should not exceed the label dose without medical advice.

### Can vitamin C help with flu or COVID-19?

The Cochrane review looked only at the common cold, so it does not show that vitamin C prevents or treats flu or COVID-19.

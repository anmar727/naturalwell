## Quick answer

Xylitol is a sugar alcohol with a sweet taste similar to sugar. It is found in many sugar-free gums, mints, lozenges and toothpastes, and it is often promoted as a natural way to prevent tooth decay.

- **It is kind to teeth compared with sugar.** Dental bodies accept xylitol as a sugar substitute that does not cause decay, so swapping sugary snacks for xylitol products is a sensible step.
- **Proof that it actively prevents cavities is weak.** A Cochrane review found only low to very low quality evidence, with a modest benefit seen mainly for fluoride toothpaste that also contains xylitol.
- **Chewing sugar-free gum after meals does help**, but much of that benefit seems to come from chewing itself, which boosts saliva, rather than from xylitol in particular.
- **It is not a replacement** for brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and cleaning between your teeth.
- **Keep it away from dogs.** Even small amounts can be life-threatening for them.

## What the research says

**The Cochrane review (2015).** Riley and colleagues looked at 10 randomised trials, published between 1991 and 2014, involving 7,969 people, of whom 5,903 were included in the analyses. Almost all were children aged from 1 month to 13 years; only one study involved adults.

The main finding came from two studies in Costa Rica with 4,216 schoolchildren. Over three years, children using fluoride toothpaste that also contained xylitol had about 13% less decay in their permanent teeth than children using fluoride-only toothpaste. The reviewers rated this evidence as low quality. For other products, such as syrups, lozenges and tablets, they found little or no evidence of benefit, and they said they were surprised by the lack of good evidence for xylitol chewing gum. Their overall conclusion was that a cavity-preventing effect of xylitol has not been proven.

**The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD).** The AAPD's policy on xylitol, last revised in 2015, describes both short-term and long-term evidence as inconclusive. It notes that many studies had design weaknesses and conflicts of interest. The AAPD still supports xylitol and other sugar alcohols as sugar substitutes that do not cause decay.

**Sugar-free gum in general.** The American Dental Association (ADA) says chewing sugar-free gum may help lower the risk of decay when added to normal brushing and cleaning. Clinical trials found less decay in people who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals. Chewing raises saliva flow many times over, and saliva helps neutralise acids and put minerals back into enamel. The ADA also notes a 2021 meta-analysis in which xylitol-sweetened gum lowered levels of *Streptococcus mutans*, one of the main decay-causing bacteria. Fewer bacteria is not the same as proof of fewer cavities.

Overall, the evidence rating here is **limited**. Xylitol is a good alternative to sugar, but claims that it actively protects teeth go further than the research supports.

## How people use it

There is no officially agreed dose of xylitol for teeth. What is known comes from how the trials were run:

1. **Amounts used in studies.** The AAPD reports that trials typically used about 4 to 15 g of xylitol a day, split into three to seven servings. Most used four or five servings a day, which the AAPD says may not be realistic for most people.
2. **Gum after meals.** The ADA and the UK's Oral Health Foundation both describe chewing sugar-free gum soon after eating or drinking, for up to about 20 minutes. Gum sweetened with xylitol is one option; gum sweetened with other sugar substitutes also stimulates saliva.
3. **Toothpaste.** The best (though still low-quality) evidence is for fluoride toothpaste that contains xylitol. The fluoride is the key ingredient, so make sure any toothpaste you choose contains it.
4. **Read the label.** Products vary in how much xylitol they contain.
5. **Keep the basics.** Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, clean between teeth once a day, and cut down on how often you have sugary foods and drinks. Dentists see xylitol as an extra, not a substitute for these habits.

> Safety note: xylitol is highly toxic to dogs. Keep gum, mints, sugar-free sweets, toothpaste and any other xylitol products well out of their reach, and contact a vet straight away if your dog eats any.

## Possible side effects and interactions

**Stomach upset.** Xylitol is not fully absorbed in the gut, so larger amounts can cause bloating, wind and diarrhoea. The Cochrane review lists these effects and notes that many trials did not report side effects well. The AAPD also mentions abdominal discomfort and diarrhoea after swallowing xylitol.

**A possible link with heart health.** In 2024, an NIH-funded study led by researchers at the Cleveland Clinic found that among more than 2,000 people, those with the highest blood levels of xylitol were about 50% more likely to have a heart attack, stroke or related event over three years than those with the lowest levels. In a small experiment with 10 healthy volunteers, a xylitol-sweetened drink raised blood xylitol levels about 1,000-fold within 30 minutes, and blood platelets became more prone to clotting while levels were high.

This kind of study shows a link, not proof that xylitol causes heart problems. The lead researcher said people do not need to throw away products such as xylitol toothpaste, but that eating or drinking products containing large amounts could raise clotting risk, and more research is needed. The concern in the study was about higher intakes, such as foods and drinks sweetened with xylitol, rather than small amounts in dental products.

## Who should be careful

- **People with heart or circulation problems,** or a history of blood clots or stroke, may want to avoid large amounts of xylitol in food and drinks until more is known, and can ask their doctor about it.
- **People with a sensitive gut,** including those who get diarrhoea easily, may notice symptoms even at moderate doses.
- **Young children.** The Oral Health Foundation does not recommend chewing gum for children under seven. Ask a dentist before giving any xylitol product to a young child.
- **People with braces or some dentures.** The Oral Health Foundation advises against gum with braces, and gum can stick to some dentures.
- **Households with dogs** (and ferrets): the FDA warns that xylitol can be dangerous for them.

## When to see a doctor

See a dentist if you have:

- toothache, or pain when biting
- sensitivity to hot, cold or sweet foods that does not settle
- visible holes, dark spots or chalky white patches on your teeth
- bleeding, swollen or receding gums

Get urgent medical or dental help for facial swelling, swelling under the jaw, fever with tooth pain, or difficulty swallowing or opening your mouth. These can be signs of a spreading infection.

See a doctor for diarrhoea that lasts more than a couple of days, contains blood, or causes signs of dehydration.

For pets: the FDA says a dog that has eaten xylitol can develop low blood sugar within 10 to 60 minutes, with vomiting, weakness, staggering, collapse or seizures. Contact a vet or emergency animal clinic immediately, even if the dog seems well, because some effects take 12 to 24 hours to appear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is xylitol better than fluoride for preventing cavities?

No. Fluoride has much stronger evidence. The best xylitol result in the Cochrane review was a small extra benefit when xylitol was added to fluoride toothpaste, not a replacement for it.

### Does xylitol gum work better than other sugar-free gum?

It is not clear. The ADA says xylitol gum reduces levels of decay-causing bacteria, but the Cochrane reviewers found little good evidence that xylitol gum prevents cavities. Much of the benefit of chewing gum seems to come from the extra saliva.

### Is xylitol safe for people?

It is widely used in toothpaste, mints and gum. Larger amounts can upset the stomach, and a 2024 study raised questions about high intake and blood clotting.

### Can my child use xylitol?

Ask your dentist first. The Oral Health Foundation does not recommend gum for children under seven, and the evidence for xylitol in young children is weak.

## Quick answer

Chia seeds are tiny seeds from *Salvia hispanica*, a plant in the mint family. Their main, well-established benefit is **fibre**: two tablespoons provide close to 10 grams, about a third of the daily recommendation for adults. That can help with constipation and keeping bowel movements regular.

They also contain plant omega-3 (ALA), protein and minerals. Claims that chia seeds dramatically lower blood sugar, blood pressure or weight come from small studies with inconsistent results.

The main safety point: **don't eat dry chia seeds followed by a drink**. They can swell rapidly and, in rare cases, have caused blockages in the throat.

## What's in chia seeds

According to US Department of Agriculture data, about 28 grams (roughly two tablespoons) of dried chia seeds contain approximately:

- **Fibre:** about 10 g, mostly insoluble, with some soluble fibre that forms a gel
- **Protein:** about 4 to 5 g
- **Fat:** about 9 g, of which roughly 5 g is alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), a plant omega-3
- **Calcium, magnesium, phosphorus and iron** in useful amounts
- **Energy:** about 140 kcal

## What the research says

### Digestion and regularity

Fibre adds bulk to stool and helps it retain water, which supports regular bowel movements. Most adults eat less fibre than recommended (around 25 to 30 g a day). Chia seeds are a convenient way to add fibre. There are few trials of chia seeds specifically for constipation, but the benefits of increasing fibre are well established.

### Blood sugar

Chia's soluble fibre forms a gel that slows digestion. Small studies have found that adding chia seeds to a meal can reduce the rise in blood sugar afterwards. Longer trials in people with type 2 diabetes show small or inconsistent effects on overall blood sugar control.

### Heart health

Some small trials found modest reductions in blood pressure, especially in people with hypertension. ALA omega-3 is linked to heart health in population studies, but the body converts only a small proportion of ALA to the omega-3s found in fish (EPA and DHA). Chia is not a substitute for oily fish for that purpose.

### Weight

Fibre and protein can increase fullness, but trials have not shown that adding chia seeds to the diet leads to meaningful weight loss on its own.

## How to eat chia seeds safely

1. **Soak them first.** Mix 1 tablespoon of chia seeds with about 4 tablespoons (60 ml) of liquid (water, milk or a plant drink) and leave for at least 10 to 15 minutes, or overnight to make chia pudding.
2. **Or mix into moist foods** such as yoghurt, porridge or smoothies, and allow a few minutes before eating.
3. **Start small.** Begin with 1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon a day and build up to 1 to 2 tablespoons over a week or two, to avoid bloating and wind.
4. **Drink plenty of water** through the day when you increase fibre.
5. **Ground chia** is easier to digest and makes nutrients more accessible, but whole seeds also work.

> Never eat a spoonful of dry chia seeds and then drink water. Chia seeds can absorb many times their weight in liquid. A reported case involved dry seeds swelling in the oesophagus and causing a blockage that needed hospital treatment. This risk is higher for people with swallowing difficulties.

## Possible side effects and interactions

- **Bloating, wind and abdominal discomfort**, especially if you increase fibre quickly
- **Constipation** if you eat a lot of fibre without enough fluid
- **Allergy**, which is rare but has been reported, sometimes in people allergic to sesame or mustard seeds
- **Blood pressure and diabetes medicines:** large amounts may add slightly to the effect; check your readings if you eat chia regularly
- **Blood thinners:** omega-3s in very large amounts might affect bleeding; normal food amounts are not a concern
- **Seeds and teeth:** small seeds can get stuck between teeth and around braces. Rinsing or flossing after eating helps.

## Who should be careful

- People with **swallowing difficulties** or narrowing of the oesophagus: only eat chia that has been fully soaked, or avoid it
- People with **inflammatory bowel disease** during a flare, or with bowel narrowing: check with your doctor before eating high-fibre seeds
- People with a **sesame or mustard seed allergy**
- Young children: give soaked chia only, in small amounts, because of choking risk

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if:

- You have **constipation lasting more than three weeks** despite more fibre and fluid, or it alternates with diarrhoea
- You notice **blood in your stool**, black stools, or unexplained weight loss
- You have **severe abdominal pain**, a swollen abdomen or vomiting
- You have **difficulty swallowing** or the feeling that food is stuck. Get urgent help if you cannot swallow saliva after eating chia seeds.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many chia seeds should I eat a day?

One to two tablespoons a day is a common, sensible amount for most adults.

### Do chia seeds need to be soaked?

Soaking is the safest way to eat them and makes them easier to digest. Sprinkling small amounts on moist food is also fine.

### Are chia seeds better than flaxseeds?

Both are good sources of fibre and ALA. Flaxseeds need grinding for the body to absorb their nutrients; chia does not. Choose the one you prefer.

### Can chia seeds help with IBS?

Some people with constipation-predominant IBS find soluble fibre helpful. Others find seeds make bloating worse. Introduce them slowly and see how you feel.
